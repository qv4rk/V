Ibrahim Pasha withdrew from Syria and Palestine in 1840, not because anyone defeated him on the ground but because Britain, Austria, Russia, and Prussia forced the issue — diplomatically at first, and then with a British naval bombardment of Acre. The Ottomans recovered their territory in early 1841. The empire that returned was not the one that had lost it nine years earlier. It was indebted, anxious about Russian expansion, and about to let a British ambassador shape a significant piece of its legislative agenda.

That ambassador was Stratford Canning, who had already served three separate postings to the Ottoman court before returning in 1841 for what became his longest and final term. He did not draft Ottoman legal text himself. What he held was leverage — specifically the Crimean War, fought against Russia from 1853 to 1856, during which the empire needed British troops, ships, and money to survive. Canning used that need to press for private property protections, equal legal rights for non-Muslims, and a European-style commercial framework, carrying memos into closed-door meetings with Mustafa Reşid Pasha and Âli Pasha, men he had known and worked alongside for nearly two decades. Reşid and Âli did the work of translating those demands into language the Sultan, Abdülmecid I, and the religious establishment could accept. The result was the Hatt-ı Hümayun of February 1856, folded into the peace terms at the end of the war.

The 1858 Ottoman Land Code followed two years later, drafted under the Tanzimat Council with Reşid Pasha as its political architect — he died in early 1858, shortly before its completion — and Ahmet Cevdet Pasha as its legal draftsman. The Code's stated purpose ran opposite to what it eventually produced: registering land directly in the names of the peasants who farmed it, replacing the old miri system of customary, unrecorded use-rights with an individual title deed, the tapu, so the state could tax cultivators directly instead of going through tax farmers and local strongmen who had skimmed the difference for generations.

When the registrars arrived in the villages with their books, the peasants who had lived through Ibrahim Pasha's conscription decrees less than two decades earlier read a government request to write down exactly who owned what as a setup for the draft, or for back-taxes they couldn't pay. Whole villages asked literate, well-connected merchants in Beirut, Damascus, Nablus, and Jerusalem to register the village lands under their own names instead. The merchants agreed. Legal title to large stretches of Palestinian agricultural land passed to a new class of urban landlords, some of whom never set foot on the land they now owned. The cultivators kept working the same fields. The deed now said someone else's name.

Mustafa Abbasi's research follows this story past the families everyone already knows — the Husseinis and Khalidis of Jerusalem, the Nimr and Touqan and Jarrar families of Nablus, the Abd al-Hadi clan of Arraba — toward the regional notables who ran the same system on a smaller stage: the Tabari family in Tiberias, whose standing traced back to Sheikh Dhaher al-Umar al-Zaydani's rule there in the early eighteenth century; the Qadoura and Nahawi families in Safed; the Fahoum family in Nazareth; the Shuqayri and Saadi families in Acre. The same salam contracts and kafalat al-wajh debt arrangements that bound a sheikh in Ein al-Zeitun to a peasant's harvest ran through these families' ledgers too — urban capital reaching into rural villages through advance crop sales structured to function as interest, collected whether or not the harvest came in.

In 1872, the Ottoman government sold the Jezreel Valley — Marj ibn Amir, some of the most fertile land in Palestine — to the Sursock family, Greek Orthodox bankers based in Beirut, for roughly £20,000. No rural clan held the title. No fellah who had farmed that ground for generations had ever owned the deed that would have let him refuse the sale. The Sursocks held the valley for decades before selling portions of it, in the 1920s, to Zionist land-purchasing organizations. The valley changed hands the way capital changes hands once the paperwork exists to allow it — registered, priced, and signed for in a city ledger, by people who had never touched the soil it described.
