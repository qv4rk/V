# War on Drugs: The Forever War at Home

Richard Nixon declared a 'war on drugs' in 1971. Fifty years later, drugs are cheaper, purer, and more available than ever. By any measure, drugs won. But the war continues—because the war itself is the product.

The necronomy ecosystem: private prisons profit from incarceration, police departments profit from civil asset forfeiture, defense contractors profit from militarizing law enforcement, rehab centers profit from court-mandated treatment, drug testing companies profit from employment screening, probation services profit from monitoring, and on and on.

Each arrest generates revenue across multiple sectors. Each conviction creates a permanent administrative relationship. Each release creates a monitoring obligation. The cycle is self-sustaining because it's designed to be.

Actual drug policy reform—decriminalization, harm reduction, treatment—threatens the entire ecosystem. Not because it wouldn't work, but precisely because it would. You can't run a permanent war without an enemy, and you can't maintain an industry built on war without perpetual conflict.

The war on drugs is necronomy at scale: create a crisis, criminalize it, then build an economy around managing the criminalization.
