Why did nobody in Rev. Malachi Duggan's parish build a better house for nine straight years running? The priest gave the Devon Commission a one-word answer, under oath, in August 1844: Crotty. The system that made one man's name sufficient explanation had a name of its own that outsiders found almost comic: mock ground. Land proprietors in County Clare called it conacre, and it worked the same way across most of Munster and Connacht — a landholder let out a strip of ground, usually already manured, for eleven months and no longer, just long enough to sow and harvest a single crop. No lease was signed, because the eleven-month term existed specifically to avoid creating one. The tenant held no legal claim to the ground he worked, no right of renewal, no protection if the same field was promised to someone else the following season. Rent ran anywhere from three pounds ten shillings to eight pounds an Irish acre, paid in cash, in labor on the landholder's own farm, or some combination of the two, and if a man fell behind, the ground's owner could recover what was owed by distraining and selling his standing potato crop, or through a civil bill process that cost him more than the debt itself. County landowner James Molony, testifying to the Devon Commission's inquiry in 1844, told the commissioners plainly that he believed the scarcity of conacre land was itself the origin of most agrarian outrage in the county — the poor, in his words, conceived that farmers and proprietors ought to make sacrifices to give them the means of subsistence they could not otherwise obtain.

The Commission had been sitting since November 1843, chaired by the Earl of Devon, taking sworn testimony from landowners, land agents, and tenant farmers across every county in Ireland on the state of the law respecting the occupation of land. On Friday, August 23, 1844, its sitting at Kilrush took evidence from a Roman Catholic parish priest named the Rev. Malachi Duggan, witness number 608 on that day's list. Duggan was asked a fairly abstract question — what effect did tenancy-at-will, as opposed to a formal lease, have on a tenant's willingness to improve his own holding. He answered with a story instead of an opinion.

He had known, he told the commissioners, many men turned out over the preceding thirty-two years, but one case in particular had done lasting damage to the whole parish. A poor man had built himself a nice dwelling-house on his holding, without a lease, because no lease was on offer. The farm was subsequently given over to a middleman, who took a fancy to the house the poor man had built with his own labor, picked a quarrel with him, and threw him out onto the highway. Asked directly for the man's name, Duggan gave it without hesitation: Crotty. Asked how long ago it had happened, he said about twelve years — placing the eviction around 1832, a full thirteen years before the Commission would publish its final report and, as it turned out, three years before a plant disease anyone in that room could have named would make every question they were asking briefly, catastrophically moot.

What followed in Duggan's testimony was the detail that gives the story its actual weight. He did not say the parish forgot Crotty. He said the opposite: no improvement was made in his parish for eight or nine years afterward, because everybody liked the house — a pretty thing, he called it, paved all around, with back offices built onto it. The dwelling itself had been the argument against building one. For the better part of a decade, men in Duggan's parish who might otherwise have put labor and whatever small capital they had into a better roof or a paved yard chose not to, having watched exactly what happened to the one neighbor who had.

Nothing about this required a villain acting in bad faith by his own lights. The middleman who took Crotty's house was operating inside a system that made his conduct not merely legal but, from where landlords like David J. Wilson sat, actively encouraged. Wilson — a Clare landowner who submitted his own model for a "cheap cottage for a cottier" to the same Commission, complete with a separate sleeping room, a warm pig-sty so the sow needn't be brought indoors to farrow, and a small room specifically built for a loom — argued elsewhere in his testimony that leases were themselves a positive bar to improvement, since a tenant secured by one had, in his telling, no reason to submit to a steward's instruction. Wilson believed he had solved the cottier's condition with drawings and a subsoil-loosening technique that cost ten to fifteen shillings an acre. He was not being cynical. He had simply never had to weigh what he'd learned to build against the reasonable certainty that it might be taken from him without cause, the way Crotty's had.

The arithmetic ran the same way at every level below the landlord. William Daxon, a farmer and land agent who testified the day before Duggan at Ennis, was asked whether small tenants preferred a lease at a high rent or land without one at a lower rent, and answered that they would rather have the lease every time — because a lease meant credit, meant a bank in Limerick or Ennis would advance a man fifteen or twenty pounds at six percent, where a tenant-at-will had no standing to borrow from anyone but a country usurer charging fifty percent interest a year. A man with no lease could not improve his land without risking exactly what Crotty lost, and could not borrow to improve it in any case, because no lender would extend credit against a holding he might not still occupy by spring.

Daniel Cullen, another Clare farmer who testified that same week, told the commissioners a different but related story: that in his own recent experience, getting land at all from an agent named White in the townland of Quinpool had cost him twenty pounds paid quietly to the agent, entered in no receipt and no deed — a sum he understood other tenants taking farms at the same time had also been made to pay. When the landlord himself, Benjamin Finch White, was given the chance to answer the accusation in an appendix to the same report, he stated flatly that he had never been aware any such payment had changed hands, and that he strongly disapproved of the practice. Both statements can be true. That was the ordinary condition of tenure in Clare in 1844 — a system opaque enough that a landlord could plausibly not know what was being extracted from his own tenants in his name, and a tenant had no forum to raise it in except a royal commission that had traveled specifically to Ennis, Scarriff, Kilrush, and Limerick to ask.

Duggan gave his answer that August afternoon in Kilrush and the Commission moved on to its next question, one witness among several hundred it would hear before the sitting ended. Nothing in the surviving record suggests anyone in that room treated Crotty's story as remarkable. It was simply one more piece of sworn testimony confirming what tenure without a lease did to a man who built something worth wanting, entered into the official minutes and set beside all the others like it — while, outside the courthouse, the parish Duggan had just described was still doing exactly what it had done for nine years running: nothing worth taking, to no one who could take it.

## Voices

**Cautious tenant-at-will · Moyarta, West Clare · c. 1832**

Three cartloads of blue flags are lying in the gripe of the ditch outside my haggard gate since the autumn before the autumn that is gone. Pried my own two boys them out of the cliff below Kilbaha with an iron crow, and drew they them home on the slide-car when the mare was idle after the oats, and tipped they them into the nettles. When I go out in the dark of the morning to look to the beasts, step I over the green puddle at the threshold—ankle-deep in the black clout of it when the rain is off the Shannon—and strikes my brogue the corner of the top flag sticking out of the weeds. I lift it not. If the boy puts a hand to the loy to level the step, take I the ash-plant to his shoulder. Let the mud come in over the door-sill; is no shine on the mud. Rides a man past on a blood-mare from Kilrush, and the polished tops on his boots and the whip across his thigh, turns he not his head in the saddle to look at a cabin that has the green water standing in the floor and the thatch rotten brown on the west gable. Had Martin Crotty a spirit-level of brass set in mahogany that he got off a ship's carpenter in Carrigaholt. Saw I him with it on my knees when he was laying the flags before his door in Kiltrellig—down on his two knees in the gravel, squinting the one eye at the little bubble of air in the glass so the rainwater would run three inches to the perch clear of the wall and into the shore. Built he the cow-byre with lime mortar, and a slate roof on the pig-house, and whitewashed he the gable-end facing the road so you would see it three miles off from the rise of Moyarta like a gull sitting on the bog. Rode Tom Pilkington by one Tuesday, and his father-in-law the agent with him, and pulled he up his cob at the white gate, and looked he at the dry yard and the clean stone, and said he to himself that a house the like of that was too dry for a Crotty. Six-and-thirty pound gave Martin into his hand in bank-notes out of an old horn cup he had buried under the hearthstone, and seven pound more at the November gale to keep the roof over the woman while the frost was on the ground. In the spring threw they the bed out on the road, and got the woman the stitch in her side under a sheet of sailcloth in the ditch, and before the cuckoo was calling in the furze, were the two of them under the clay in Moyarta churchyard. Fourteen shillings in silver and two pound-notes of the Provincial Bank are sewed into the lining of my old frieze waistcoat behind the left armpit, where the sweat of my body keeps the paper damp and tough. There it stays. Walked I into the bank in Kilrush without a lease in my hand, would the clerk behind the mahogany rail look through me as if a pane of glass was in me. Went I to Pat Keane the mealman for the loan of five pound till the harvest, would he write seven pound ten on the stamp-paper before he wet the pen. So when comes the rain through the scraws over the bed, move we the tick three feet to the east and set we the iron pot under the drip. A notice to quit is a dry floor.

**Young conacre labourer · Tulla, East Clare · 1831-1844**

Pares a man the sod with the breast-spade, three inches thick, taking the roots of the heather and the old tough bent-grass with it, and shoves he with the front of his thighs against the cross-hilt until the skin over the hip-bones is chafed raw and bleeding into the wool of his drawers. Two weeks was I at the paring of that half-acre on Moloney's hill, and another week setting the sods up on their edges like little roofs to dry in the March wind. When we put the coal of turf into the heart of the heaps, hung the smoke of them low over Tulla for four days, blue and sour, and the taste of burned worms and dead roots in it. Four pound ten has the farmer chalked down against my name on the back of his kitchen door—four pound ten for the bare skin of a hill that would not feed a snipe, and neither a creel of dung out of his yard nor the loan of an ass to draw lime from the kiln given to me. How pays a man four pound ten on eightpence a day when only ninety days' hiring is in the twelve months of the year? With your back in the August sun you pay it, while your own ridges are choking with the redshank, and with the potatoes out of the clay you pay the balance before ever your childer put a tooth in them. In the pit are they now—sixty barrels of Lumpers covered over with a foot of yellow clay and beaten flat with the back of the spade. An iron padlock is on the hurdle-gate of the field that the farmer bought in Ennis for eighteenpence. My own potatoes, dug with my own loy out of ashes I burned with the push of my own groin. Last night boiled my woman a handful of nettles and a fist of charlock tops, and three small chats the size of marbles that she scratched out of the headland with her nails after the dark. Sat the five of us round the skib and drank we the green water off them to stop the griping in our guts, while sixty barrels of my own growing lay locked under the frost three fields away because eleven shillings short on the tally-stick am I. In the year of the Terry Alts, before I was married to her, went four of us over the wall into O'Grady's dairy meadow at two in the morning, and the bandages of white calico tied round our hats and the shirts pulled out over our breeches. Touched we not a beast of his. Took we the four-prong forks and the long-handled loys, and turned we the green sod of that bullock-pasture upside down—two rood of it before the moon went behind the cloud—and left we it black and broken so no bullock could bite a blade on it for three years, and he forced to let it out in mock-ground to the boys for the spade. Light and strong were the two arms on me that night; would I have tossed aocking of stones over the chapel roof. Now stand I by the gripe of the road in the rain, looking through the bars of a gate at the mound where my winter's meat is buried. Break I the farmer's lock, will he have me on the chain-gang in Ennis jail before the week is out.

**Evicted Clare woman in a cellar · Thomond Gate / Mass Lane, Limerick · 26 August 1844**

Count the feet when the candle is out and knowledge will be at you why turns no soul over in his sleep. Two of my sister's boys at the wall and their heads to the door; my own three across the middle and their shins under my chin; my sister and myself sitting and our backs propped against the salt-barrel; and my husband stretched along the outer edge where comes the wet in under the bottom step of the stairs from Mass Lane. Four yards one way and three yards the other, and so low is the ceiling that when goes a dray by on the flags outside, sifts the dust of the rotten beam-timber down into our open mouths. Brought we the two-stone pot with us on the ass-cart when they tumbled the gable in Cratloe, thinking a pot was a thing would always have use in it. Upside down in the corner sits it now for my father to sit upon while the cough is on him. What would we put in it? Boils no man a fire on the flags of a cellar that has no chimney in it, ach a hole knocked through the foundation-stone into the channel of the street. When we light a handful of bog-fir to warm the sup of buttermilk, hits the smoke the arch of the roof and rolls it down on top of us until the childer are vomiting black phlegm on the straw. Wait till the January and see what is eaten at us. Half-a-penny at the stall by Thomond Bridge is the price of a salt herring—one red herring and the eyes sunk white in its head and the yellow oil oozing out of its gills. Takes my mother a piece of spun yarn and ties she that one fish by the tail to the joist over the table. When the potatoes are turned out on the kish, takes each of us a potato in his fist and rubs he the hot skin of it the one time—ach the one rub against the greasy belly of the herring to give the smell of the sea to the potato—and eats he it dry. For a week hangs the fish there until is no heart in anyone to rub it anymore, and then boil we the bones of it in the water. Down the steps on the Tuesday came Mr. Cullen the grocer, and the Parliament gentleman behind him. Was a great tall beaver hat on the gentleman, and brushed the crown of it a cobweb off the joist the minute he stepped off the ladder, and saw I the shudder go down through his shoulders before ever his boots touched the wet clay of the floor. Was a white cambric handkerchief in his gloved hand, and held he it up to his mouth, not quite touching his nose, for he had no wish to be seen doing it out of politeness to the poor. Went his eyes round the dark of the wall to where my little girl was sitting naked on the turf-kish, and the scald-head crusted yellow over her ears. Asked he never a question. Backed he up the five steps and his face to us, the way backs a man out of a stall when lays a vicious horse its ears flat, and when went his head out into the daylight, heard I him spit on the pavement in the street.
