The Ledger and the Mob: Galilee Under the Pashas, 1831-1840

Ibrahim Pasha's conscription and disarmament decrees gut a province's old bargain between service and exemption. The peasants revolt; the revolt curdles into a pogrom in Safed and a massacre at Hebron; a village called Ein al-Zeitun shelters Jewish refugees while some of its own sons join the violence next door.

In 1831, Ibrahim Pasha marched his army into Greater Syria in the name of his father, Muhammad 'Ali Pasha, ending a century of Ottoman rule by proxy. Governance had been outsourced for decades to local strongmen — Ahmad al-Jazzar Pasha in Acre, Muhammad Abu Nabbut in Jaffa — men who ran their own fiefdoms with their own muscle and tax arrangements. Ibrahim Pasha arrived with a modernizing army — the nezzam — and a centralized bureaucracy built to replace that system with direct rule from Cairo. But as historian Judith Mendelsohn Rood details from the Jerusalem Islamic court registers (sijillat), this was more than an administrative shift; it was a direct assault on the established Sunni legal order. For generations, Ottoman subjects lived under an understood contract of "repose and tranquility" — a reciprocal balance where Muslim elites managed endowments (waqfs), local sheikhs provided defensive military levies, and non-Muslim communities (ri'aya) paid the jizya tax in exchange for physical protection and exemption from military service. Ibrahim Pasha dismantled the contract at both ends. His administration seized control of the Waqf al-Haramayn (the holy endowments of Mecca and Medina), curbed the authority of the Sunni ulama, imposed a universal head tax (farda), and stripped away the customary toll and portage fees that Bedouin tribes and the Abu Ghosh family had long collected along the pilgrimage routes.

On April 25, 1834, the tension reached an ultimatum. Ibrahim Pasha summoned the leading notables and scholars of Palestine to Jerusalem. Sitting across from him were the city's highest religious and administrative authorities: the Mufti Tahir Afandi al-Husayni, the Naqib al-Ashraf 'Umar Afandi al-Husayni, and the chief court clerk (Bashkatib) Muhammad 'Ali al-Khalidi, alongside regional leaders like Qasim al-Ahmad, sheikh of Nablus, and 'Isa al-Amr of Hebron. Ibrahim laid out his demands: two hundred conscripts from Jerusalem to start, thirty-five hundred from the Jerusalem and Nablus districts combined, and five hundred from Hebron. As recorded by the contemporary chronicler Spyridon, Ibrahim pressed them directly: "As we, Muslims, have as perpetual enemies the Nazarene nations, is it or is it not necessary for us to have a big standing army?" The notables agreed with the principle, but rejected his method. "There is no need for us to give up our boys and young men for war," they replied. "When the enemy of our religion enters our country, all of us, young and old, will go out and fight and willingly shed our blood..." Ibrahim dismissed their tradition out of hand: war was not for a "herd of useless men," but required permanent, professional conscripts forcibly inducted for life.

The two sides were describing the same word — service — and meaning two entirely different social contracts. For a Muslim household, military service had been an episodic duty to defend one's home. Now, Ibrahim Pasha was demanding their sons forever, disarming the peasantry, and leaving the Muslim population to bear the full "blood debt" of lifetime conscription while non-Muslim neighbors paid redirected taxes to Cairo. Desperate men began blinding themselves in one eye or cutting off their fingers to escape the draft. By May, the countryside detonated in coordinated revolt. Qasim al-Ahmad organized the uprising from his seat in Beit Wazan, joined by 'Abdullah al-Jarrar of Sanur and 'Isa al-Barqawi of Shufa. In the Hebron hills, 'Isa al-Amr led the highland clans; in Bani Zeid, 'Abd al-Jabir Barghouti called out the villages; around Acre and Haifa, Mas'ud al-Madi commanded the coastal rebels; and in the Galilee, Aqil Agha led Hawwara irregulars fighting alongside local sheikhs. Ten thousand armed peasants surrounded Jerusalem. Days later, an earthquake shook the city, which the rebels interpreted as divine judgment against Ibrahim Pasha's draft.

On May 19, locals from Silwan showed thirty-six rebels an abandoned sewer pipe running under the Dung Gate (Bab al-Maghariba). Crawling through the conduit on May 20, they threw open the gates and let thousands of armed peasants stream into Jerusalem. The Egyptian commander, Rashid Bey, withdrew his troops into the inner Citadel (qala'a), arresting Mufti Tahir Afandi al-Husayni, 'Umar Afandi al-Husayni, and Bashkatib Muhammad 'Ali al-Khalidi as hostages. Two thousand Nablus fighters forced the Damascus Gate. Command and control rapidly dissolved. As the Greek Orthodox monk Neophytus recorded, while peasant sheikhs explicitly ordered the crowd not to harm Christian and Jewish subjects (ri'aya) for fear of drawing European military intervention, low-class bandits and enraged mobs overran the quarters regardless. Shops were smashed, floors dug up in search of hidden silver coins, and commercial quarters looted.

In Safed, where local governance had collapsed completely, the turmoil found a scapegoat. On June 15, 1834, a local preacher named Muhammad Damoor incited a crowd with claims of treasure in the Jewish quarter. For thirty-three days, the quarter was systematically plundered. Israel Bak's printing press — the only Hebrew press operating in the region — was wrecked. Fourteenth-century manuscripts belonging to Rabbi Isaac Aboab I were destroyed, and a hiding rabbi was blinded in one eye. Yet, inside that same violence sat the intricate reality of human behavior. The chronicler Isaac Farhi recorded Rabbis Mendel of Kamenetz, Leib Cohen, and Shalom Hayyat walking through the wreckage to bury the dead and reunite separated children. And twelve-year-old Jacob Saphir spent forty days hiding safely in the neighboring Arab village of Ein al-Zeitun, sheltered by an unnamed local sheikh — even while some of that same village's young men joined the assault on Safed. This was the tragic mechanism of displaced rage: a peasantry stripped of its sons and disarmed by an invading state took vengeance on its neighbors, while individuals within those same communities risked their lives to grant sanctuary.

On June 30, Muhammad 'Ali Pasha landed at Jaffa with fifteen thousand fresh troops to reinforce his son. Ibrahim Pasha rejected all truce terms mediated by Mufti Tahir Afandi and 'Isa al-Amr. Qasim al-Ahmad issued a final call to arms: "Fight bravely for your homes and your honour, for your rights and especially for your beloved children." The Khedival army crushed the insurgency with total force. Jerusalem's top leadership — Mufti Tahir Afandi, 'Umar Afandi, Bashkatib Muhammad 'Ali al-Khalidi, Shaykh 'Abdullah Budayri, and Muhammad Abu Saud — were chained, exiled, and imprisoned in Egypt. Mas'ud al-Madi and his son 'Isa were beheaded at Acre.

In early August, Ibrahim Pasha targeted Hebron, where Qasim al-Ahmad had made a final stand. Egyptian troops stormed the city and subjected it to a brutal sack. Five hundred Muslims were killed in heavy street fighting, and despite assurances of protection, twelve Jewish residents were killed in what became remembered as Yagma el Gabireh — the Great Destruction. Qasim al-Ahmad fled across the Jordan to al-Karak, but after Ibrahim Pasha besieged the town and destroyed its orchards, the Bedouin chief Duwaikhi al-Samir surrendered him. Qasim was executed in Damascus alongside Arsab al-Kahol and 'Isa al-Barqawi. His sons Yusuf and Muhammad were beheaded at Acre.

When the blood cleared, the regional balance of power had been remade. Jabr Abu Ghush, who had defected to the Egyptian side, was appointed mutasallim of Jerusalem, while the allied Abd al-Hadi clan assumed control of Nablus. Thousands of young local men were dragged off in chains to serve in Egypt's distant wars. History recorded the catastrophic failures of 1517, 1628, 1660, and 1834, preserving the paper trails of crisis while leaving unrecorded the quiet decades when the local security arrangement held. And on January 1, 1837, a massive earthquake leveled Safed to rubble, burying thousands under the stone homes they had fought so desperately to keep.
