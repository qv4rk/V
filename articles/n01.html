The Ledger and the Mob: Galilee Under the Pashas, 1831-1840

Ibrahim Pasha's conscription and disarmament decrees gut a province's old bargain between service and exemption. The peasants revolt; the revolt curdles into a pogrom in Safed and a massacre at Hebron; a village called Ein al-Zeitun shelters Jewish refugees while some of its own sons join the violence next door.

In 1831, Ibrahim Pasha marched his army into Greater Syria and ended a century of Ottoman rule by proxy. Governance there had been outsourced for decades to local strongmen — Ahmad al-Jazzar Pasha in Acre, Muhammad Abu Nabbut in Jaffa — men who ran their own fiefdoms with their own muscle and their own tax arrangements, answering to Istanbul mostly in name. Ibrahim Pasha arrived with a modernizing army and a centralized bureaucracy built to replace that system with direct rule from Cairo. The new administration came with conscription quotas, requisition orders, and a tax structure that bypassed local intermediaries entirely. It also began stripping income from the people who had been collecting it informally for generations: Muhammad Ali's officials took over the administration of religious endowments across Syria, curbed the authority of the local ulama, and ended the portage and toll fees that Bedouin tribes and the Abu Ghosh family had long collected along the roads near Jerusalem.

On April 25, 1834, Ibrahim Pasha summoned the leaders of Jerusalem and Nablus and laid out what he needed: two hundred conscripts from Jerusalem to start, thirty-five hundred more from the Jerusalem and Nablus districts combined, five hundred from Hebron. He asked the notables directly — since they were Muslims at war with Christian nations, wasn't a real standing army a necessity? They didn't disagree with the principle. They told him their men were already trained fighters, the same as their fathers before them, ready to shed blood defending their land from religious enemies when the moment required it. That wasn't the same thing Ibrahim Pasha was asking for, and he told them so: he wanted professional, permanent soldiers, not what he called a herd of useless men called up only when trouble arrived at the door. The two sides were describing the same word — service — and meaning two different arrangements by it.

For a Muslim peasant household, the old arrangement had run a particular way: a man's sons served when called, and the neighbors who didn't fight paid a tax instead, the jizya, while whatever clan or family controlled the roads nearby added its own trickle of income from the tolls. Egyptian centralization left the costly half of that arrangement untouched — the sons still went to war — while dismantling the half that had offset it, redirecting the Abu Ghosh family's toll income and the Bedouin tribes' portage fees into the new treasury in Cairo. The jizya itself wasn't abolished; Jews and Christians went on paying it. But the old bargain had never just been about a tax on someone else's ledger. It was about the rest of the local economy that had grown up around military service being the price one community paid and exemption being the price the other one paid instead — and only one side of that arrangement was now being touched.

By May, the peasantry had stopped waiting to find out how this would resolve. On May 19, the notables of Jerusalem and Nablus told Egyptian officials they could no longer conscript anyone or collect the new taxes, because the men of the surrounding villages had taken up arms and fled into the mountains, out of reach. Qasim al-Ahmad, sheikh of the Jamma'in subdistrict near Nablus and recently removed from his post, organized the revolt that followed from his home in Beit Wazan, joined there by Abdullah al-Jarrar of Sanur and Isa al-Barqawi of Shufa. In the Hebron hills, the fighting was led by Isa al-Amr; in Bani Zeid, by Abd al-Jabir Barghouti. Around Acre and Haifa, the rebels were commanded by Mas'ud al-Madi. Ten thousand men from Nablus, Hebron, Jerusalem, and Gaza converged on Jerusalem and tried to take it by force in early May. In the Galilee, Aqil Agha — who had defected out of Ibrahim Pasha's own service — led Hawwara irregulars fighting alongside unnamed local sheikhs near Safed. Days after the siege of Jerusalem began, an earthquake struck the city and damaged infrastructure across the region, on top of everything else already in motion.

Safed, already strained by the unrest, took the political chaos and the aftershocks together. On Sunday, June 15, 1834 — the day after Shavuot — a local preacher named Muhammad Damoor, according to accounts later relayed to the British traveler Alexander Kinglake, told a crowd that true believers should rise up and take the gold and silver of the Jewish community. For thirty-three days, until July 17, the Jewish quarter was looted, burned, and desecrated. Later estimates cite around 500 Torah scrolls and manuscripts destroyed. Bak's printing press — at the time the only Hebrew press operating in the region — was wrecked, though Israel Bak rebuilt it in Jerusalem within a few years. Fourteenth-century volumes belonging to Rabbi Isaac Aboab I were lost. A rabbi who hid in a cave was found and blinded in one eye. Isaac Farhi, who survived the violence and died in 1853, left one of the clearest firsthand accounts of it — and also recorded Rabbi Mendel of Kamenetz, and in some accounts Rabbis Leib Cohen and Shalom Hayyat, walking through the wreckage during the chaos to reunite separated children with their parents and bury the dead. Yisroel ben Shmuel of Shklov, who led what remained of the Jewish community, wrote to the foreign consuls in Beirut asking for intervention. Ibrahim Pasha eventually sent the Druze emir Bashir Shihab II with Egyptian and Druze troops to restore order; a researcher named Löwe later put the Jewish community's material losses at 135,250 piastres.

No letter survives from any man who took part in those thirty-three days explaining what he thought he was owed and from whom. What survives instead is the shape of what had happened to him in the months before it: his sons conscripted or in hiding from conscription, his weapons confiscated, the toll income his family might have lived on for generations redirected to a treasury in Cairo he would never see a coin from, and then a man standing up in front of him with a date, a target, and the suggestion that none of it had been entirely fair to begin with. Whatever he told himself in the moment, it didn't have to be invented out of nothing. The grievance was real. The target was not the source of it.

What also survives, because he wrote it down himself, is what the thirty-three days felt like from the other direction. Jacob Saphir was twelve. He didn't write about conscription decrees or toll income. He wrote about hunger — three days of it, before the village next door worked out how to hide and feed that many extra people without being caught — and about a sheikh he never learned the name of, who took that risk anyway.

A number of Safed's Jewish families fled to Ein al-Zeitun, the Arab village beside the city, and were taken in. Saphir hid there for roughly forty days under the protection of the village's sheikh. No source names him. Mustafa Abbasi's research on Safed and the Galilee centers on the later Ottoman period and the Mandate; Mahmoud Yazbak's work on the sharia court registers covers Haifa, Jaffa, and Nazareth more than rural Safed in this exact decade. A name may exist in the Safed sijill for the Hijri years 1249 and 1250, in a kafalat al-wajh or a property settlement filed after the unrest — that is a real possibility, not a confirmed one. According to the same body of research, some of the village's own young men joined the assault on Safed during the same unrest, which left Ein al-Zeitun's leadership exposed to Ibrahim Pasha's reprisals once the revolt was put down. Shelter and participation came out of the same village, in the same month.

The revolt was crushed without much restraint. The worst of it came at Hebron in early August, where Qasim al-Ahmad had fled after his dismissal and reached an agreement with the local sheikhs to keep fighting. Egyptian troops broke the city's resistance in heavy street fighting, and once inside, with permission to plunder, did not distinguish carefully between combatants and civilians: roughly five hundred people were killed in the sack of the city, the great majority of them Muslim rebels and residents, against Egyptian losses of around 260. Twelve Jews were also killed there, despite Ibrahim Pasha's prior assurance that the Jewish quarter would be spared — a massacre Hebron's Jewish community remembered afterward as Yagma el Gabireh, the great destruction. This was a different mechanism than Safed: not a peasant mob targeting a specific community on a specific date, but a punitive army sacking a rebellious city and treating everyone inside it as the enemy.

Qasim al-Ahmad himself escaped to al-Karak, where a Bedouin chief named Duwaikhi al-Samir sheltered him — until Ibrahim Pasha destroyed the town and uprooted its orchards in response, after which al-Samir handed him over. Qasim was executed in Damascus alongside his lieutenant Arsab al-Kahol and Isa al-Barqawi. His sons Yusuf and Muhammad were executed separately, at Acre. The Abu Ghosh and Abd al-Hadi clans, who had defected to the Egyptian side partway through the revolt, were given official posts once it was over — the same Abu Ghosh family whose toll income had been one of the things taken from them three years earlier.

On January 1, 1837, an earthquake leveled Safed almost completely, killing by some estimates more than half its population. Foreign travelers — William Thomson, the German prince Hermann von Pückler-Muskau — wrote accounts of the devastation. Edward Robinson, passing through the valley near Ein al-Zeitun not long after, described the town as rubble from a distance, but noted that the vineyards and olive groves around it were still being tended.
